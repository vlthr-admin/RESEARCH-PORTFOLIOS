## 2.1 Concept and Core Dimensions of Operating Models

### 2.1.1 Defining the Operating Model

An operating model describes how an organisation structures its activities, resources, and responsibilities to deliver its products or services. At its most fundamental level, an operating model determines which activities are performed within the organisation's own boundary and which are obtained from external parties (Espino-Rodríguez & Padrón-Robaina 2006). This distinction shapes the distribution of control, the configuration of resources, the nature of organisational dependencies, and the structure of risk exposure.

Three broad configurations are recognised in the academic and practitioner literature. In an **in-house** model, the organisation retains direct ownership of the resources — employees, equipment, technology, and management systems — required to perform a given activity. The organisation thereby assumes full operational and financial responsibility for that activity. In an **outsourced** model, the activity is contracted to an external provider who delivers the function using its own resources and under the terms of a commercial agreement. In a **hybrid** model, the activity is shared between internal and external parties, with elements of both direct control and contractual governance operating simultaneously (McIvor 2009).

Each configuration has different implications for how the organisation acquires, deploys, and loses resources over time. These differences in resource configuration are a primary reason why operating model choices carry risk consequences that extend well beyond simple cost calculations.

### 2.1.2 In-House Operations

In-house operations allow an organisation to retain direct authority over the execution of an activity. This includes control over staffing levels, quality standards, process design, maintenance schedules, and investment priorities. Research consistently identifies direct control, quality assurance, and knowledge retention as the principal motivations for internalising activities (Tsay, Gray, Noh & Mahoney 2018).

However, internalisation also concentrates responsibility. An organisation operating predominantly in-house must provide the necessary human capital, physical capital, and infrastructure independently. This creates exposure to risks associated with internal capacity constraints, specialist skill scarcity, dependency on key personnel, and the financial burden of maintaining competence across a broad range of functions. These exposures are structural rather than incidental: they arise from the configuration of the operating model itself.

### 2.1.3 Outsourced Operations

Outsourcing enables an organisation to access capabilities, resources, and technology without the investment required to develop and maintain them internally. External providers may offer specialised expertise, economies of scale, and flexibility that would be costly or time-consuming for a client organisation to replicate in-house. Prominent reviews of outsourcing identify cost reduction, capability access, and increased flexibility as the dominant stated motivations for externalising activities (Espino-Rodríguez & Padrón-Robaina 2006).

External provision can increase the client's dependence on the provider's performance, capacity and continuity arrangements. The client generally retains responsibilities for defining requirements, monitoring delivery, managing the commercial relationship and responding to service disruption. The degree of direct operational control retained by the client depends on the activity, contract and governance arrangements (Williamson 2008).

### 2.1.4 Hybrid Arrangements

Hybrid arrangements attempt to combine the benefits of in-house control and outsourced capability. In practice, they introduce a coordination layer that neither purely in-house nor purely outsourced models require. When activities are split across internal and external actors, responsibility boundaries may become ambiguous, information flows across the organisational boundary must be actively managed, and failures must be attributed within a more complex accountability structure (Hoecht & Trott 2006).

The literature treats hybrid arrangements as genuinely distinct from either pure model rather than as a simple midpoint between them. The coordination demands, accountability risks, and governance complexity that characterise hybrid delivery represent a qualitatively different risk profile requiring dedicated management attention.

## 2.2 Theoretical Perspectives on Make-or-Buy Decisions and Governance

### 2.2.1 Transaction Cost Economics

Transaction Cost Economics (TCE), developed by Williamson, provides the foundational economic rationale for make-or-buy decisions. TCE posits that the governance of any economic activity — whether through internal hierarchy, external markets, or hybrid contractual forms — should be selected to minimise the total costs associated with the transaction. These costs extend beyond the direct price of production to include search costs, contracting costs, monitoring costs, enforcement costs, and the costs of adapting agreements when circumstances change (Williamson 2008).

Transaction cost analysis considers characteristics such as asset specificity, uncertainty and transaction frequency when assessing the relative costs and capabilities of governance arrangements. Asset specificity concerns investments that are difficult to redeploy to other uses. Uncertainty makes it harder to specify and adapt contracts, while transaction frequency affects the costs of organising repeated exchanges. These characteristics do not produce an automatic rule that internalisation is always preferable. They inform the comparative assessment of market, hybrid and hierarchical governance arrangements (Williamson 2008).

The TCE perspective is directly relevant to operating model risk analysis because it explains why low-price outsourcing may conceal substantial hidden costs. If a critical outsourced activity involves highly specific assets, the client may find that switching or replacing the supplier is significantly more costly than anticipated, creating lock-in and bargaining power asymmetry.

### 2.2.2 Resource-Based View

The Resource-Based View (RBV) treats the organisation as a collection of valuable, rare, inimitable, and non-substitutable resources and capabilities. From this perspective, the make-or-buy decision is not simply about cost minimisation but about the strategic management of organisational competence. Activities underpinned by strategically distinctive capabilities should generally be retained in-house; activities that are more generic or in which external providers possess superior capability may be candidates for outsourcing (Espino-Rodríguez & Padrón-Robaina 2006).

The RBV draws attention to the strategic value of capabilities and organisational learning. Outsourcing an activity that contributes to distinctive knowledge or capability development may weaken the organisation's future competence if knowledge transfer and retention are not managed. Internal provision can support the retention of tacit knowledge, although it does not guarantee that knowledge will be retained or developed. The decision therefore depends on the activity's strategic role and the organisation's ability to govern external knowledge and capability relationships (Espino-Rodríguez & Padrón-Robaina 2006; Hoecht & Trott 2006).

McIvor (2009) explicitly argues that TCE and RBV are complementary rather than competing perspectives on make-or-buy decisions. Transaction-cost analysis identifies when the market is likely to be economically efficient; resource-based analysis identifies when market procurement may compromise strategic capability. Both dimensions are necessary for a full assessment of operating model risk.

### 2.2.3 Resource Dependence Theory

Resource Dependence Theory (RDT) examines how organisations manage dependencies on resources controlled by external actors. Organisations may respond to such dependencies through strategies that manage uncertainty, access and power in inter-organisational relationships. The degree of dependence is shaped by the importance of the resource and the availability of alternatives (Pfeffer & Salancik 1978).

In the context of operating model risk, RDT explains why supplier dependency is a structural feature of outsourcing rather than simply a contract management challenge. When an organisation outsources an activity that requires specialised assets, niche expertise, or proprietary technology, the supplier may control resources that the client cannot readily substitute. This creates concentration risk: a single supplier failure can disrupt operations in ways that the client organisation cannot independently resolve.

RDT also clarifies the strategic value of maintaining internal capabilities even when they are more expensive than market alternatives. An organisation that retains some internal capacity preserves a credible outside option that moderates supplier bargaining power and reduces vulnerability to supply disruption.

### 2.2.4 Agency Theory

Agency Theory examines relationships in which a principal delegates work or decision authority to an agent and where interests, information and risk preferences may differ. In outsourcing, the client and provider may face information asymmetry and different incentives. Monitoring, contractual design and incentive arrangements can be used to address these problems, although their effectiveness depends on the task and relationship (Jensen & Meckling 1976; Eisenhardt 1989).

Outsourcing may involve information asymmetry because the provider often has more immediate knowledge of its own processes and effort than the client. Such asymmetry can create monitoring and incentive problems. The concepts of adverse selection and moral hazard describe possible agency problems, but the current survey does not measure whether either occurred in the participating relationships. Monitoring and verification requirements should be considered as part of the governance cost of outsourcing (Jensen & Meckling 1976; Eisenhardt 1989).

Agency theory is particularly relevant to the governance dimension of operating model analysis: it explains why effective outsourcing requires more than a contract, and why monitoring effectiveness is a critical determinant of the realised risk profile under an outsourced model.

## 2.3 Operational Risk, Quality, and Process Control

### 2.3.1 Operational Risk in Operating Model Contexts

Operational risk refers to the potential for losses or disruptions arising from failures in internal processes, people, systems, or external dependencies. In the context of operating model choice, operational risk manifests differently depending on whether activities are executed in-house, outsourced, or through hybrid arrangements (Rachalawar & Mishra 2026).

Under an in-house model, the primary operational exposures include failures of internal capacity — staffing shortages, equipment breakdowns, skill scarcity, and dependency on key personnel whose absence can interrupt critical processes. These risks are entirely within the organisation's sphere of direct management, but are also entirely the organisation's responsibility to resolve. The organisation cannot transfer the consequences of an internal staffing shortage to an external party.

Under an outsourced model, the organisation is partially insulated from the day-to-day operational demands of the activity but exposed to the supplier's performance. Delivery failures, quality deviations, and capacity shortfalls at the supplier level propagate directly into the client's operations. The client's ability to detect, diagnose, and correct supplier-side problems is constrained by the organisational boundary and by the information asymmetry inherent in principal-agent relationships (Lockamy & McCormack 2010).

### 2.3.2 Quality and Process Control

Quality and process control represent a central risk dimension in operating model comparisons. In-house operations allow the organisation to implement and enforce quality standards directly, through internal inspection, process design authority, and real-time supervisory oversight. Deviations from standard can be identified and corrected within the same organisational hierarchy.

In outsourced operations, quality assurance becomes a contractual and monitoring challenge. The client must define quality requirements with sufficient precision to make them contractually enforceable, invest in supplier audit and performance review mechanisms, and establish corrective action protocols for identified deficiencies. Research on supply network risk identifies quality monitoring, supplier audit frequency, and contract specificity as critical governance practices that moderate outsourced quality risk (Lockamy & McCormack 2010).

Under hybrid arrangements, the quality interface between internal and external components introduces additional complexity. When a production or service process spans the boundary between in-house and outsourced execution, quality failures must be attributed to either party, and the standards applied by each side must be aligned. Misalignment at the interface can produce defects that neither party regards as individually responsible for, creating a structural accountability gap.

### 2.3.3 Capacity, Flexibility, and Disruption Risk

Operational continuity depends not only on quality but on the capacity to absorb demand variation and recover from disruption. In-house operations provide direct control over capacity allocation and investment, but their capacity is constrained by the organisation's own capital and workforce. Scaling internal capacity in response to demand surges may require investment timelines that exceed market response requirements.

Outsourced models can, in principle, offer greater scalability by drawing on the supplier's broader resource base. However, this scalability is conditioned on the supplier's actual available capacity at the time it is needed, which may be constrained by the supplier's other commitments, its own internal disruptions, or market-wide resource scarcity. Research on outsourcing in manufacturing contexts identifies supplier capacity limitations and delivery lead-time variability as persistent operational risks that intensify during periods of external disruption (Tsay et al. 2018).

## 2.4 Strategic Risk, Knowledge Retention, and Asset Specificity

### 2.4.1 Knowledge Loss and Capability Erosion

A distinctive risk of outsourcing that the operational perspective does not fully capture is the gradual erosion of internal organisational knowledge. When an activity is outsourced, the knowledge required to perform it — including the tacit, experience-based knowledge held by former employees — may cease to be maintained within the client organisation. Over time, the client may lose the ability to evaluate the quality of the supplier's work, to specify technical requirements with precision, or to re-insource the activity if circumstances change (Hoecht & Trott 2006).

This process represents a strategic risk with a long development horizon. In the short term, the outsourcing client may observe no deterioration in performance or understanding. Over an extended period, however, the progressive atrophy of internal technical competence can make the organisation more dependent, less informed, and less capable of managing its external providers effectively. The risk is particularly acute for activities that involve proprietary technology, complex engineering knowledge, or operational expertise that is not readily available in the external labour market.

### 2.4.2 Asset Specificity and Supplier Lock-In

Asset specificity, introduced in the context of TCE, also carries strategic implications that extend beyond transaction cost analysis. When an outsourced activity requires the external provider to invest in assets — equipment, training, software, or infrastructure — that are specifically designed for the client's requirements, both parties become mutually dependent. The supplier has limited alternative uses for its specific investment; the client has limited alternative suppliers capable of delivering the bespoke requirements. This bilateral lock-in creates vulnerability to opportunistic behaviour by either party and increases the difficulty of contract renegotiation or exit (Williamson 2008).

The strategic risk is compounded when dependence is asymmetric — when the client's dependence on the supplier is greater than the supplier's dependence on the client. In such conditions, the client may face price escalation, reduced service levels, or supply disruption without an effective remedy, because the cost and time required to develop alternative supply arrangements are prohibitive.

### 2.4.3 Information Security and Intellectual Property Risk

Outsourcing that involves the transfer of technical designs, production processes, proprietary data, or operational know-how to an external provider creates exposure to information leakage risk. The external provider, by gaining access to valuable proprietary information in the course of service delivery, acquires knowledge that may be used for its own competitive purposes, shared with other clients, or exposed through security failures (Hoecht & Trott 2006).

This risk dimension is particularly significant for activities that are closely connected to competitive advantage, such as product design, specialised manufacturing processes, and customer data management. In-house operations substantially mitigate this category of risk by retaining sensitive knowledge within the organisational boundary.

## 2.5 Governance Mechanisms, Monitoring, and Interface Management

### 2.5.1 Contractual Governance

Effective governance of outsourced activities requires a contractual framework that defines service specifications, quality standards, key performance indicators, escalation procedures, and exit terms with sufficient precision to align the supplier's behaviour with the client's requirements. Research in supply chain risk management identifies contract completeness and specificity as important moderators of outsourcing risk (Lockamy & McCormack 2010).

However, contracts are inherently incomplete in the presence of uncertainty. When operating environments change in ways that were not anticipated at the time of contracting, the client and supplier must either renegotiate terms or absorb the consequences of contractual gaps. The costs of renegotiation and the risks of opportunistic behaviour during renegotiation are among the central concerns of TCE (Williamson 2008).

### 2.5.2 Performance Monitoring and Supplier Management

Beyond contractual design, effective outsourcing governance requires active operational monitoring. Performance monitoring mechanisms — including regular performance reviews, key performance indicator tracking, on-site audits, and corrective action processes — provide the client with the information necessary to detect supplier-side problems before they escalate into service disruptions. The monitoring gap, defined as the difference between the client's desired level of operational visibility and its actual ability to observe supplier performance, represents a structural feature of all outsourcing relationships (Hoecht & Trott 2006).

Effective supplier management programmes reduce the monitoring gap through systematic data collection, supplier relationship investment, and regular structured communication. Research on supply network risk management identifies multi-supplier strategies, supplier risk assessment, and contingency planning as the governance practices most strongly associated with operational resilience in outsourcing-dependent organisations (Lockamy & McCormack 2010).

### 2.5.3 Interface Management in Hybrid Arrangements

Hybrid operating models introduce a specific governance challenge: the management of the boundary between internal and external execution. When responsibility for a process is divided between in-house teams and external providers, the handoff points — where outputs pass from one party to the other — require explicit definition and active management. Information that is self-evident within a single organisation must be formally communicated, verified, and accepted across the hybrid boundary.

Research on hybrid arrangements consistently identifies role ambiguity and coordination failure as the characteristic risk exposures of split delivery models. When accountability is unclear, failures at the interface may not be reported, investigated, or corrected promptly, creating a structural tendency toward quality degradation and delay at the boundary.

## 2.6 Organisational Resilience, Continuity, and Recovery Capabilities

### 2.6.1 Defining Resilience in Operating Model Contexts

Organisational resilience describes the capacity of an organisation to absorb disruption, maintain critical functions, and restore normal operations following an adverse event. In the context of operating model risk, resilience depends on the availability of alternative delivery options, the speed with which problems can be detected, and the effectiveness of recovery responses (Rachalawar & Mishra 2026).

Operating model choice has direct implications for all three components of resilience. The ability to detect problems depends on the design, coverage and timeliness of monitoring. In-house arrangements may provide direct access to operational information, while outsourced arrangements may require information to pass through contractual and organisational interfaces. Neither structure guarantees effective or ineffective detection; the outcome depends on the actual monitoring and information-sharing arrangements. The availability of alternative delivery options depends on whether the organisation maintains redundant internal capacity, multiple suppliers, or the contractual right to insource activities when required.

### 2.6.2 Business Continuity Planning and Operational Redundancy

Business continuity planning involves the advance design of response procedures, recovery resources, and communication protocols to be activated when a significant operational disruption occurs. In the context of outsourced operations, continuity planning must address the specific scenario of a supplier failure, which differs fundamentally from an internal operational disruption in terms of the client's degree of control over the recovery process.

Key continuity measures applicable to outsourcing-dependent operations include the maintenance of alternative or backup supply arrangements, safety stocks of critical materials, contractual provisions for emergency re-insourcing or rapid supplier substitution, and documented recovery procedures that have been tested under realistic conditions (Lockamy & McCormack 2010). The absence of such measures substantially extends the recovery timeline following a supplier disruption.

### 2.6.3 Resilience Trade-Offs Across Operating Models

The literature does not support the conclusion that any single operating model is uniformly more resilient than another. In-house operations provide strong detection capabilities and direct recovery authority, but resilience may be constrained by internal capacity limits and the difficulty of rapidly scaling resources without external support. Outsourced operations can draw on a supplier's broader resource base, but only if the supplier's own continuity is maintained and the contractual framework includes appropriate provisions.

Hybrid models may offer a combination of internal detection speed and external capacity flexibility, but only when the governance and coordination structure is sufficiently robust to maintain clear accountability and information flow during a crisis. The evidence suggests that the realised resilience of any operating arrangement depends less on the structural model than on the governance quality, planning depth, and recovery investment associated with it (Rachalawar & Mishra 2026).

## 2.7 Conceptual Framework and Analytical Sequence

### 2.7.1 Integrating the Theoretical Perspectives

The theoretical perspectives reviewed in the preceding sections address complementary aspects of operating model risk. Transaction Cost Economics explains why governance structure affects the total costs and risks of an activity, with particular attention to asset specificity, uncertainty, and contracting challenges. The Resource-Based View explains why outsourcing decisions must consider the strategic value of internal capabilities and the long-term risks of capability erosion. Resource Dependence Theory explains why supplier dependency constitutes a structural source of vulnerability that persists across the life of an outsourcing relationship. Agency Theory explains why information asymmetry between client and supplier creates monitoring costs and governance challenges that are not captured by the surface price of outsourcing.

No single theory provides an adequate account of operating model risk in isolation. McIvor (2009) demonstrates that TCE and RBV must be considered jointly to explain outsourcing decisions and their consequences. Tsay et al. (2018) similarly show that outsourcing in production and operations management encompasses financial, quality, innovation, and strategic dimensions simultaneously. The integrated perspective required for the present study therefore draws on all four theoretical traditions.

### 2.7.2 The Analytical Chain

Based on the reviewed literature, the relationship between operating model choice and organisational risk can be represented through the following analytical chain, which forms the structural basis for the empirical investigation in subsequent chapters:

> **Operating Model → Resource Configuration → Control Distribution → Dependency Structure → Risk Exposure → Consequences → Response and Recovery → Residual Risk**

Under an in-house model, the organisation directly provides and governs the activity's resources. It may retain closer operational authority, while remaining exposed to internal capacity, skill, equipment and continuity constraints.

Under an outsourced model, the provider performs the contracted activity using resources defined by the arrangement. The client retains responsibilities for requirements, contract governance and oversight; the level of direct operational control varies with the contract and service design. Supplier dependency and monitoring requirements are potential risk exposures.

Under a hybrid model, resources and responsibilities are distributed across internal and external parties. Coordination, information exchange and accountability at organisational interfaces are therefore important areas for assessment; their effectiveness depends on the actual governance design.

### 2.7.3 Research Gap and Contribution of the Present Study

The reviewed literature provides substantial theoretical and empirical insight into the drivers and consequences of outsourcing decisions. However, a specific gap is relevant to this study. Much existing research examines outsourcing within a single industry or functional domain, treating either in-house or outsourced operation as the baseline comparator. Fewer studies examine all three models — in-house, outsourced, and hybrid — simultaneously, under a comparative framework that addresses both operational and strategic risk dimensions in an integrated manner (Tsay et al. 2018).

Existing reviews also find that empirical evidence on outsourcing outcomes is inconsistent across contexts, with financial, quality, strategic, and innovation consequences varying considerably by sector, activity type, and governance quality (Strange & Magnani 2017). This contextual sensitivity reinforces the need for comparative empirical evidence examining practitioner assessments of risk distribution across operating model configurations.

The present study applies the analytical chain established in this chapter to questionnaire responses from professionals assessing a specific operating activity. The study offers an exploratory comparison of respondent assessments across in-house, outsourced and hybrid arrangements. The scope of the literature search should be kept in view when describing the contribution; the present review does not establish that no comparable framework exists elsewhere.
