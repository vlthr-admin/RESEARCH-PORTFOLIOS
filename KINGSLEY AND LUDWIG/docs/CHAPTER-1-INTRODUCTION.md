# 1 Introduction

## 1.1 Background of the Study

Companies continuously make decisions about how their operations should be organised. One of the most consequential decisions concerns whether a given activity should be performed within the organisation or obtained from an external service provider. Activities such as production, maintenance, logistics, procurement, information technology, and other support functions can be organised through in-house operations, outsourcing, or a combination of both. Each arrangement reflects a different allocation of resources, responsibilities, and control, and each carries a different configuration of risk.

The choice between in-house and outsourced operations affects more than the location where work is performed. It can influence the resources available to the organisation, the distribution of operational control, the degree of dependence on external parties, the retention of organisational knowledge, the mechanisms required for monitoring and governance, and the organisation's ability to respond to disruption. Consequently, the operating model chosen can materially affect the organisation's risk profile.

In-house operations allow an organisation to retain direct responsibility for resources and activities. However, internal operations also require the organisation to provide the necessary employees, equipment, skills, infrastructure, management, and capital. These requirements can create operational and business exposures that are structural in nature. Outsourcing can provide access to external capabilities and resources, but it can also introduce dependencies on suppliers and create a need for additional contractual, monitoring, and coordination mechanisms.

The risk implications of different operating arrangements are not, therefore, adequately represented by a simple distinction between low-risk and high-risk models. A change from in-house to outsourced operations may reduce one type of exposure while introducing or increasing another. For example, an organisation may reduce its internal resource requirements while simultaneously increasing its dependence on supplier performance. Similarly, retaining an activity internally may provide greater direct control while increasing the organisation's responsibility for capacity, staffing, maintenance, and investment.

This creates a need to examine operating models through a broader risk perspective. The present thesis focuses on two complementary dimensions. The first is the operational dimension, which concerns the functioning and continuity of day-to-day activities. The second is the strategic and business dimension, which concerns longer-term issues such as cost exposure, organisational capabilities, external dependency, knowledge retention, scalability, and strategic flexibility.

The thesis therefore examines how the choice of operating model is associated with the distribution and structure of organisational risk. The focus is not on establishing which operating arrangement is inherently preferable, but on identifying how different operating configurations create, retain, transfer, reduce, or amplify different forms of risk.

## 1.2 Research Problem

The central research problem is that organisations must make operating model decisions under conditions of uncertainty, while simultaneously managing both operational requirements and broader strategic consequences.

An organisation may outsource an activity to obtain specialised capabilities, reduce internal resource requirements, or increase operational flexibility. At the same time, the organisation may become dependent on an external provider and find it necessary to establish mechanisms for monitoring, coordination, performance management, and continuity. Conversely, an organisation may retain an activity internally in order to maintain direct control and preserve organisational knowledge, while accepting the resource, capacity, investment, and operational responsibilities associated with internal execution.

The operating model decision therefore has the potential to change the structure of organisational risk rather than simply increasing or decreasing it as a whole. An organisation that outsources to reduce one category of risk may find that a different category of risk has been introduced or amplified as a consequence.

Existing theory offers several perspectives for understanding these relationships, including transaction cost analysis, capability-based reasoning, resource dependence, and agency relationships. However, these perspectives have not always been applied together in a comparative framework that addresses in-house, outsourced, and hybrid arrangements under both operational and strategic risk dimensions. Furthermore, much of the existing empirical research focuses on specific industries or functions, limiting the generalisability of its conclusions.

The research problem addressed in this thesis can consequently be expressed as follows:

*How do in-house and outsourced operating models affect an organisation's risk profile?*

This problem is examined by identifying the risk dimensions associated with each operating model and analysing how responsibility, control, resources, dependencies, and recovery capabilities are configured under different arrangements.

## 1.3 Purpose of the Study

The purpose of this thesis is to examine how in-house, outsourced, and hybrid operating models affect an organisation's risk profile from both operational and strategic perspectives.

The study seeks to develop a structured basis for comparing the risks associated with different operating arrangements. It considers not only the risks that may arise from outsourcing but also the risks that are retained or created when activities are performed internally. Similarly, it examines how hybrid arrangements — in which activities are shared between internal and external parties — produce a distinct risk profile that is not simply an average of the two pure models.

The intended outcome of the study is an evidence-based understanding of how operating model choices influence the distribution of organisational risk. The research also aims to provide a comparative framework that can support the assessment of operating model decisions, by connecting operating arrangements with resource configuration, control distribution, dependency structures, risk exposure, disruption consequences, and response and recovery capability.

## 1.4 Objectives of the Study

The objectives of this thesis are to:

1. examine the operational and strategic risks associated with in-house operations;
2. examine the operational and strategic risks associated with outsourced operations;
3. compare control, monitoring and accountability across in-house, outsourced and hybrid operations;
4. examine how internal resource and external provider dependencies relate to continuity, response and recovery; and
5. identify factors influencing the choice and continued use of in-house, outsourced and hybrid operating models.

## 1.5 Research Questions

The central research question is:

*How do in-house and outsourced operations affect a company's risk profile?*

The supporting research questions are:

- **RQ1:** What operational and strategic risks are associated with in-house operations?
- **RQ2:** What operational and strategic risks are associated with outsourced operations?
- **RQ3:** How do control, monitoring and accountability differ across in-house, outsourced and hybrid operations?
- **RQ4:** How do internal resource and external provider dependencies affect continuity, response and recovery?
- **RQ5:** What factors influence the choice and continued use of an in-house, outsourced or hybrid operating model?

Use these exact questions and numbering in every project document. The questions are descriptive and comparative. They do not presume that the operating model causes a particular outcome or that one model is universally preferable.

## 1.6 Scope and Delimitations of the Study

The study focuses on the relationship between operating model choice and organisational risk profile. The operating models considered are in-house, outsourced, and hybrid arrangements, as described by the respondents in the context of the specific operational activity they assessed.

Two risk dimensions are examined. The operational dimension includes areas such as process control, quality, reliability, capacity, specialist skills, continuity, supplier performance, and technology dependency. The strategic and business dimension includes areas such as cost exposure, strategic dependency, knowledge retention, core competence, scalability, bargaining power, intellectual property, and financial exposure.

The study does not attempt to evaluate the financial performance outcomes of operating model choices in aggregate, nor does it provide a normative prescription that one model should be universally preferred over another. The study is descriptive and comparative: it examines reported professional assessments of risk exposure and distribution across the three operating model categories.

The empirical component is a cross-sectional questionnaire study using purposive, non-probability recruitment. The questionnaire did not request respondents’ names. The response count is 30 respondents; the data do not establish that each respondent represents a unique organisation. The current analysis dataset contains 30 respondent records, distributed across Finland and eleven further European countries, and covering in-house ($n = 9$), outsourced ($n = 12$), and hybrid ($n = 9$) operating arrangements. The study does not claim to constitute a statistically representative probability sample of European industrial organisations. Its findings are interpreted as exploratory comparative evidence, appropriate for the academic scope and objectives of a bachelor's thesis.

## 1.7 Structure of the Report

The report is organised as follows. Chapter 2 establishes the theoretical knowledge base, reviewing the academic literature on operating models, make-or-buy theory, operational and strategic risk, governance mechanisms, and organisational resilience, and presenting the conceptual framework that guides the empirical investigation. Chapter 3 describes the research methodology, including the research approach, unit of analysis, operationalization of variables, sampling strategy, data collection instrument, and analytical methods. Chapter 4 reports on the implementation of the study, covering questionnaire construction, pilot testing, recruitment, data collection administration, data quality verification, and dataset preparation. Chapter 5 presents the empirical results, organised according to the three operating model groups and the cross-model comparative analyses. Chapter 6 discusses the findings in relation to the theoretical knowledge base, addresses the research questions, evaluates the validity and reliability of the study, identifies limitations, and presents conclusions and recommendations for practice and further research.
