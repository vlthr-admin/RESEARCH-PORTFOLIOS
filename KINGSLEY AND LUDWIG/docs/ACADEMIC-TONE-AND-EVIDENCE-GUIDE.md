# Academic Writing and Evidence Standard
## Karelia UAS thesis: A Comparative Analysis of How In-House and Outsourced Operations Affect a Company’s Risk Profile

**Purpose:** Define the writing, evidence, interpretation and citation standards to be applied consistently across the thesis and its supporting documents.

## 1. Required academic tone

The thesis should use a formal, clear, neutral, evidence-led and critically analytical academic tone. It should communicate what the research examines, what sources establish, what respondents reported and what conclusions can reasonably be drawn. Academic tone is not created by complicated vocabulary or long sentences. It is created by precision, transparent reasoning, correct attribution, balanced interpretation and a clear distinction between evidence and inference.

Use first-person plural (we) when describing decisions and actions taken by the two researchers. Use neutral constructions when reporting general knowledge or empirical results.

### 1.1 Core characteristics

| Characteristic | Required practice | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Formal | Complete, grammatically correct sentences and professional terminology. | Slang, contractions and conversational phrasing. |
| Neutral | Describe evidence without promoting a preferred operating model. | Loaded, persuasive or promotional wording. |
| Precise | Name the activity, model, variable, group and denominator. | Vague claims about companies or industries. |
| Evidence-led | Cite theory and trace empirical claims to the dataset. | Unsupported assertions or decorative citations. |
| Critical | Explain limitations and plausible alternative explanations. | Treating one result as proof of a theory. |
| Concise | Include information that advances the research question. | Repetition, inflated introductions and decorative language. |
| Consistent | Use the same questions, concepts, labels and scales throughout. | Switching terms as if they were interchangeable. |
| Transparent | Separate verified actions from plans and unresolved matters. | Claiming approvals or checks without records. |

## 2. Evidence boundaries

Classify substantive statements as one of four types:
1. **Source-derived knowledge:** concepts or findings reported in an academic or authoritative source. Cite using Karelia’s method.
2. **Dataset result:** a count, percentage, rating or pattern calculated from questionnaire data. State the group, variable and denominator.
3. **Researcher interpretation:** an explanation offered by us. Signal it as interpretation and identify the evidence supporting it.
4. **Project-process fact:** an approval, recruitment, pilot, data-handling or implementation statement. Include it only when supported by a record or explicitly identified as a researcher-reported fact.

A source may support a theoretical explanation but cannot prove that the same mechanism occurred among the 30 respondents. A questionnaire rating records a respondent’s assessment; it does not independently verify actual performance, incident frequency or causal effects.

Preferred formulations include: “The literature suggests”; “Respondents in this sample rated”; “The descriptive results indicate”; “One possible interpretation is”; “This pattern is consistent with”; “The available data do not establish whether”.

Avoid “proves”, “confirms”, “causes”, “all companies”, “the industry clearly”, “definitive answer” and “statistically significant” unless the relevant evidence and method genuinely support the statement.

## 3. Statistical and comparative language

The study uses a purposive, non-probability sample of **30 respondents**. The questionnaire did not collect respondent names and did not establish that every respondent represented a unique organisation. Therefore:
- Write “30 respondents” or “30 questionnaire responses”, not “30 organisations”.
- Do not infer a count of unique companies from the response count.
- Describe results as sample-specific descriptive findings; do not claim population representativeness.
- Do not claim statistical power, population estimates or causal effects.
- State subgroup denominators whenever percentages are reported.
- For multi-select questions, explain that percentages may exceed 100%.
- Distinguish a structurally blank conditional-branch field from an unanswered question that was presented.

### 3.1 Preferred rephrasing

| Overstated | Preferred |
|---|---|
| “In-house operations have superior detection.” | “The in-house group reported a higher mean problem-detection rating in this sample.” |
| “Hybrid operations provide better recovery.” | “The hybrid group recorded the highest mean recovery rating among the three groups in this sample.” |
| “Outsourcing causes a monitoring deficit.” | “The outsourced group reported a lower mean monitoring-effectiveness rating than the in-house group reported for internal control effectiveness. The items measure related but non-identical constructs.” |
| “This confirms the theory.” | “This pattern is consistent with the theoretical expectation, although the study design cannot test the causal mechanism.” |
| “All hybrid organisations face coordination failure.” | “All nine respondents in the hybrid subgroup selected coordination failure as the primary hybrid risk option.” |

Report means with medians and, where appropriate, dispersion. Rating scales are ordinal in character. Avoid treating small numerical differences as meaningful without context. Do not use “advantage”, “deficit”, “hierarchy” or “superior” as unqualified conclusions.

## 4. Interpretation structure

Use this sequence for each important result:
1. State the result with count or statistic and denominator.
2. Describe the comparison and identify the relevant groups.
3. Relate the pattern to the knowledge base.
4. Offer a bounded interpretation, not a causal conclusion.
5. State what the data cannot establish.

Example: “Respondents in the in-house group rated internal skill dependency at a mean of 8.89 out of 10 (n = 9). This indicates that respondents generally perceived the assessed activities as highly dependent on internal specialist skills. The result is consistent with resource-dependency concerns discussed in the literature. However, the questionnaire did not measure actual staffing shortages or quantify the consequences of losing a specific employee.”

## 5. Terminology and construct discipline

- **Operating model:** arrangement through which the assessed activity is delivered.
- **In-house:** activity delivered predominantly using the organisation’s internal resources.
- **Outsourced:** activity delivered predominantly by an external provider.
- **Hybrid:** internal and external parties both contribute, or delivery varies materially by task or location.
- **Risk exposure:** extent to which uncertainty may affect the activity or organisational objectives.
- **Dependency:** reliance on a resource, person, system, provider or relationship.
- **Control:** ability to direct, monitor or influence an activity.
- **Monitoring effectiveness:** respondent’s assessment of the ability to monitor an external provider.
- **Accountability:** clarity over responsibility for decisions, actions and outcomes.
- **Continuity:** ability to maintain the activity during disruption.
- **Recovery:** ability to restore the activity after disruption.
- **Resilience:** capacity to anticipate, prepare for, respond to and adapt to disruption.

Do not combine risk exposure, control effectiveness, continuity and accountability into one score. They are related but distinct constructs.

### 5.1 Cost harmonisation

Combine the original make-or-buy labels **Cost** and **Total cost** under **Cost and financial considerations**. Preserve the original labels in the raw data. In the harmonised analysis, count each respondent once if either or both labels were selected. Do not add category frequencies blindly where respondents could select both labels. Calculate the unique respondent count from response-level data.

### 5.2 Control harmonisation

Combine **Control** and **Direct control** under **Control and operational authority**. Preserve original labels in the raw data and count each respondent once if either or both were selected. This is a make-or-buy decision factor, not a rating of control effectiveness. Keep IH2, OS2, HY1–HY3 and D3 as separate measures; they assess different control, monitoring, coordination or accountability constructs.

### 5.3 Optional open-text field

The optional additional-insight field is supplementary. Do not claim qualitative analysis unless usable text responses are present and a documented coding procedure is applied. If no usable text is available, state that no qualitative analysis was conducted and report the structured questionnaire results only.

## 6. Methodology and ethics wording

Describe the study as a cross-sectional, questionnaire-based exploratory and descriptive comparison using purposive, non-probability recruitment. The supervisor has given the researchers permission to proceed. No formal ethics-review decision is recorded in the project information supplied for this revision. Do not call supervisor permission “formal ethics approval”. Do not claim a research permit, formal exemption, GDPR compliance determination or institutional data-protection approval unless documentation supports it.

The questionnaire did not request respondents’ names. This does not, by itself, establish complete anonymity. Consider whether combinations of country, role, industry and company-size information could indirectly identify a respondent. Describe the consent mechanism, participant notice, storage and retention arrangements only from the actual documents and configuration used.

## 7. Karelia UAS citation and reference method

Karelia’s thesis reporting instructions govern the citation style. Do not substitute APA or another style.

### 7.1 In-text citations

- One author: (Williamson 1981, 548) when a specific page is available.
- Two authors in brackets: (Espino-Rodríguez & Padrón-Robaina 2006, 49).
- Two authors in the sentence: Espino-Rodríguez and Padrón-Robaina (2006, 49) explain…
- For three or more authors, list all surnames in the first citation; later citations may use the first surname plus et al., as Karelia specifies.
- When author names are part of the sentence, use “and”, not an ampersand.
- Put multiple sources in alphabetical order by author surname, separated by semicolons, only when they support the same point.
- Add page numbers for specific claims or quotations when stable pagination exists.
- Do not put URLs in in-text citations.

### 7.2 Reference list

Use the heading **References**. Include only sources cited in the thesis and list them alphabetically by author surname or organisational author.

- **Journal articles:** author(s), year, article title, journal title, volume, issue and page range or article number. Include DOI or stable URL and access date when applying Karelia’s electronic-source requirements.
- **Books:** author, year, title, publication location and publisher.
- **Edited-book chapters:** chapter author, year, chapter title, editor(s), book title, publisher location, publisher and page range.
- **Standards:** standard identifier and year, full title and issuing organisation, following Karelia’s example.
- **Legislation:** official act title and number/year; include relevant section and subsection in the in-text citation.
- **Electronic institutional sources:** author or organisation, year of publication/update, title, organisation where applicable, full URL and access date. If no publication/update year exists, apply Karelia’s retrieval-year rule.

### 7.3 Source verification

Prioritise peer-reviewed journal articles and academic books. Relevant standards, legislation and authoritative institutional publications are also acceptable where they are the appropriate primary source. Verify that each publication exists; confirm author names, year, title, journal or publisher, volume, issue, pages and DOI/URL; read the source or an adequate full text; confirm that it supports the claim; record page numbers where available; and ensure that each in-text citation matches a reference entry. Do not invent metadata or page numbers. Do not include uncited sources.

## 8. Chapter-level standards

- **Chapter 1 – Introduction:** define the problem, relevance, purpose, objectives, definitive research questions, scope and report structure. Do not present results or claim a research gap without literature support.
- **Chapter 2 – Knowledge Base:** synthesise, compare and evaluate literature. Organise the discussion around concepts needed to answer the research questions. Avoid disconnected source summaries. Explain how the literature informs the analytical framework and questionnaire.
- **Chapter 3 – Methods:** explain what was actually done and why. Separate completed procedures from plans. Do not claim validation, reliability, representativeness or ethics approval without evidence.
- **Chapter 4 – Implementation:** report the process using verifiable dates, records and outputs. Mark undocumented details for confirmation rather than inventing them.
- **Chapter 5 – Results:** report data without explaining causes. Give counts, denominators and percentages. Explain missing and not-applicable values. Do not claim statistical significance without justified inferential analysis.
- **Chapter 6 – Discussion:** interpret findings against the knowledge base and research questions. Address alternative explanations and limitations. Conclusions must follow from reported evidence.

## 9. Paragraph and sentence quality

A strong analytical paragraph contains a clear opening statement, evidence or a citation, an explanation of relevance, a qualification where needed, and a link to the research question. Prefer one main idea per paragraph. Define specialist terms at first use. Use consistent British English unless the programme specifies otherwise. Keep tables and figures close to the text that introduces them and explain their relevance.

Avoid excessive bolding, rhetorical questions, marketing language, unsupported superlatives, unnecessary acronyms and long chains of nouns. A theoretical framework is not proof of an empirical result.

## 10. Final quality-control checklist

- [ ] The central question and five supporting questions match across all project documents.
- [ ] The sample is described as 30 respondents, not 30 organisations.
- [ ] No unsupported claims of representativeness, causation or statistical significance.
- [ ] Every statistic has a traceable variable, numerator and denominator.
- [ ] Cost and control decision-factor labels are harmonised once per respondent, without double counting.
- [ ] Harmonised decision factors remain separate from effectiveness ratings.
- [ ] Structural branch blanks are distinguished from missing applicable answers.
- [ ] Open-text claims are supported by usable text and a documented method.
- [ ] Supervisor permission is not described as formal ethics approval.
- [ ] Methodology and implementation statements reflect documented events.
- [ ] Citations and references follow Karelia’s instructions.
- [ ] Chapter 5 reports; Chapter 6 interprets.

**Controlling principle:** Write only what the source, dataset or project record supports. Where evidence is incomplete, state the limitation or leave a clearly marked verification item.
